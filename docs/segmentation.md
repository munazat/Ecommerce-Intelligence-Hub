# Customer segmentation

**Question:** which customers are worth retaining, and what should we do differently for
each group?

**Answer:** five clusters at the 2011-09-01 snapshot. One group — 20.5% of customers —
generates 72.9% of lifetime revenue and carries the lowest churn. The largest group is not
"loyal": it is 1,949 mid-value accounts cooling toward lapse, and it is the only large
population where a reactivation sequence has positive expected value. The bottom 30.9% of
customers are already lapsed and one-time; they should be suppressed from paid channels,
not discounted.

Everything below is generated by `uv run python -m hub.analytics.segment_report` and
committed in `reports/segments.json` and `reports/figures/`.

---

## 1. Population and clock

The segmentation uses the point-in-time store, not the raw facts. One customer appears
exactly once, and every feature is history-only by construction.

| Decision | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Snapshot | **2011-09-01** | Latest snapshot whose 90-day label window (to 2011-11-30) is fully observed. 2011-10/11/12 snapshots are retained in the warehouse but censored. |
| Customers with history | 5,314 | Everyone with at least one identified line before the snapshot. |
| Excluded | 63 | Refund-only accounts (`frequency = 0`): no purchase invoice, zero monetary value, zero product diversity. They would form an artefact cluster, not a market segment. |
| **Segment population** | **5,251** | |
| Active in the prior 90 days | 1,923 | Churn is defined only here. |
| Lapsed at the snapshot | 3,328 | Already inactive; churn is not defined for them. |
| Single-purchase customers | 1,586 (30.2%) | No observed inter-purchase interval; imputed with tenure (see below). |

Segmenting the whole base rather than only the active customers is deliberate. Recency is
a segmentation axis, and the lapsed base is the win-back pool. Reporting a single "churn
rate" for a segment that is 97% lapsed would be meaningless, so churn is measured only
among active customers and lapsed segments are described by reactivation and forward value.

## 2. Features and preprocessing

| Feature | Role | Raw skew | After `log1p` |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| `recency_days` | R — days since last purchase | 0.69 | -0.90 |
| `frequency` | F — purchase invoices | 11.02 | 1.06 |
| `gross_revenue` | M — lifetime gross revenue | 23.82 | 0.24 |
| `tenure_days` | Extra — days since first purchase | -0.63 | -2.54 |
| `avg_days_between_orders` | Extra — purchase cadence | 1.33 | -0.82 |
| `return_rate` | Extra — cancellation line share | 5.19 | 5.19 (not transformed) |
| `distinct_products` | Extra — product diversity | 6.10 | -0.33 |

- **Gross revenue, not net.** Gross is non-negative by construction. Net is negative for
  26 customers (-£32,188 in total) because refunds exceed purchases; using it would make
  `log1p` undefined and would conflate value with refund behaviour. Refunds are carried
  by `return_rate` instead, and 42.4% of the population has a non-zero return rate.
- **`log1p`, not Box-Cox.** Recency, frequency, tenure, return rate and product counts
  contain exact zeros, where Box-Cox is undefined. `log1p` halves the worst tail
  (revenue skew 23.8 → 0.24) and then `StandardScaler` puts every feature on the same
  scale. Tenure becomes left-skewed after the transform because the data window bounds it
  at 639 days; that is a real ceiling, not a modelling artefact.
- **Return rate is only standardised.** It is bounded in [0, 1] and zero-inflated.
  Transforming it does not remove the zero mass, so the honest treatment is to leave the
  scale alone and read it as a rate.
- **Single-purchase interval imputation.** 1,586 customers have no second purchase, so
  there is no interval to average. It is imputed with `tenure_days` — also elapsed time
  since the first purchase — and flagged in the customer-level table as
  `inter_purchase_imputed`. The alternative, dropping the column, would delete cadence
  information for 30% of the base; the alternative of imputing the median would tell the
  model that one-time buyers behave like repeat buyers.

## 3. Model comparison

K-Means, Gaussian mixture (full covariance) and Ward agglomerative were fitted for
k = 2..8 on the same scaled matrix. Silhouette is the primary statistic; Davies-Bouldin
and Calinski-Harabasz are reported as corroboration.

| Algorithm | k | Silhouette | Davies-Bouldin | Calinski-Harabasz | Smallest cluster |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| K-Means | 2 | 0.300 | 1.260 | 2,344 | 40.9% |
| K-Means | 3 | 0.321 | 1.217 | 2,015 | 12.6% |
| **K-Means** | **4** | **0.335** | **1.051** | 1,927 | 2.5% |
| **K-Means** | **5** | **0.281** | **1.085** | 2,038 | 2.4% |
| K-Means | 6 | 0.275 | 1.200 | 1,858 | 2.3% |
| K-Means | 7 | 0.235 | 1.232 | 1,750 | 2.3% |
| Ward | 3 | 0.291 | 1.128 | 1,704 | 5.6% |
| Ward | 4 | 0.302 | 0.983 | 1,542 | 1.0% |
| Ward | 5 | 0.203 | 1.279 | 1,460 | 1.0% |

What the table actually says:

- **GMM is not competitive.** Its best silhouette is 0.225 at k=2 and it falls to 0.112
  by k=5. BIC keeps improving as k rises because the covariance structure absorbs the
  continuous features, but the partition is not compact in the scaled space. BIC is a
  density criterion, not a segmentation criterion; it is shown in the JSON, not used to
  pick k.
- **Ward is close but produces unusable small clusters.** At k=4 and above it puts ~1% of
  customers (about 50 people) into a cluster. That is a distinct micro-group, not a
  segment anyone can staff a campaign around, so it fails the 2% size floor.
- **K-Means is the only family with an actionable solution at every k.** The 2% floor
  still leaves k=4 through k=8 eligible.

## 4. Why the shipped model is k=5, not the statistical optimum

k=4 wins silhouette (0.335 vs 0.281) and Davies-Bouldin (1.051 vs 1.085). It is not
shipped, because of what the fourth cluster does to the lapsed base:

| k=4 cluster | Customers | Active | Median recency | Median gross | Share of lifetime revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| High-value regulars | 1,935 | 64.8% | 51 d | £2,737 | 85.4% |
| Lapsed block | 2,571 | 8.0% | 301 d | £397 | 10.3% |
| New / one-time | 616 | 72.6% | 52 d | £417 | 2.6% |
| Returns-heavy lapsed | 129 | 12.4% | 275 d | £405 | 1.7% |

The 2,571-customer lapsed block is not cleanly one population. Cross-tabulating the k=4
labels against the shipped k=5 labels shows it contains **1,605 of the 1,623 lapsed
one-time buyers** (98.9%) together with **966 of the 1,949 cooling mid-value accounts**
(49.6%); the remaining 857 cooling accounts are folded into the high-value cluster. So
k=4 does not merely add a cluster — it scatters cooling mid-value across two clusters
while burying almost every suppression candidate in the same block. Those two groups need
opposite treatments: one is a suppression list, the other is the only large reactivation
opportunity. k=5 separates them and also isolates the returns-heavy group as its own
segment. The full cross-tab is `reports/tables/statistical_choice_composition.csv` and
`statistical_choice_composition` in `reports/segments.json`, so the claim is computed
rather than asserted.

The cost is 0.054 silhouette and +0.035 Davies-Bouldin. k=6 raises Davies-Bouldin by 14%
(1.200) and splits the cooling base at a boundary with no operational meaning, so the
search stops at five. This is a documented judgement call, not a claim that k=5 is
statistically optimal. The full curve is in `reports/figures/segmentation_model_selection.png`
and every candidate is in `reports/segments.json`.

## 5. Stability

The chosen K-Means solution was refitted on 100 bootstrap samples of the scaled matrix
(duplicate indices removed, so Ward and the probabilistic models are scored on the same
footing). Agreement with the full fit is measured by Adjusted Rand Index:

| Statistic | ARI |
|---|---:|
| Mean | 0.973 |
| Median | 0.975 |
| 10th percentile | 0.956 |
| Worst of 100 | 0.928 |
| Refits below 0.5 | 0 |

The partition is stable. The worst refit still reproduces 93% of the pairwise assignment
structure. This is expected for a solution driven by two strongly separated axes (recency
and value), and it means segment membership is not an artefact of one random start.

## 6. Segment profiles

Churn is computed **only among active customers**. Lapsed segments have very few active
customers (53 and 16), so their churn column is reported for completeness but should not
be read as a precise rate.

| Segment | Customers | % base | % lifetime revenue | % next-90d revenue | Churn among active | Active share | Median recency | Median invoices | Median lifetime gross | Median products |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Champions | 1,079 | 20.5% | 72.9% | 70.6% | 16.3% | 87.1% | 24 d | 12 | £4,501 | 149 |
| Cooling mid value | 1,949 | 37.1% | 19.6% | 17.3% | 44.8% | 26.3% | 181 d | 4 | £1,083 | 56 |
| Lapsed low value | 1,623 | 30.9% | 3.9% | 4.1% | 67.9% | 3.3% | 325 d | 1 | £260 | 16 |
| New / one-time | 474 | 9.0% | 1.9% | 7.4% | 45.6% | 84.6% | 38 d | 1 | £386 | 23 |
| Returns-heavy accounts | 126 | 2.4% | 1.7% | 0.6% | 68.8% | 12.7% | 270 d | 2 | £411 | 8 |

Forward revenue is the realised revenue in the 90 days after the snapshot, not a forecast.
It is used here as a value check, not as a prediction.

### How the names were earned

Names are generated by a deterministic rule set in `name_segments`, using population
quartiles: recency p25 = 48 d and p75 = 317 d; invoice p75 = 6; gross p25 = £317 and
p75 = £2,058; tenure p25 = 307 d. Every name can be traced to a median crossing one of
those lines:

- **Champions**: 87.1% active, median recency 24 d (≤ p25), 12 invoices (≥ p75), £4,501
  (≥ p75), 149 products. This is the wholesale/regular core, not a retail "loyalty tier".
- **Cooling mid value**: only 26.3% active, median recency 181 d, gross between the
  quartiles. The name describes the state, not a guess about intent: most of the group is
  already inactive, but a quarter still buys.
- **Lapsed low value**: 3.3% active, 325 d recency, one invoice, £260 (≤ p25), 16 products.
  The long tail of one-and-done buyers.
- **New / one-time**: 84.6% active, median tenure 73 d (≤ p25), one invoice, £386. Recent
  first purchase, no repeat yet.
- **Returns-heavy accounts**: median return rate 33.3% (the population median is 0), only
  8 products, 12.7% active. A small, distinct remediation group rather than a value tier.

No segment is named "At-Risk Champions" or "Lost Customers" without evidence: the names
come from the medians in the table above, and the rule set is tested.

## 7. Rule-based RFM baseline: what ML adds, and what it does not

The baseline is a classic quintile RFM: recency, frequency and monetary value each scored
1–5, mapped to eight named rule segments. It is deterministic, explainable and free. The
two partitions agree at **ARI 0.374** — related, but not interchangeable.

Where they agree:

- 88% of the ML Champions are also rule-based Champions; 69% of Lapsed low value are
  rule-based Hibernating; 63% of New / one-time are rule-based New / promising.

Where they diverge:

- The rules split the active mid-value base across Champion/Loyal/Core/Occasional and
  produce four segments with **no active customers** ("At risk", "At risk — high value",
  "Hibernating" and "Occasional"; 2,495 customers, 47.5% of the base). Their churn rate is
  therefore undefined, not zero. The ML partition keeps the cooling base as one actionable
  group and separates lapsed from active by construction.
- ML segment churn spans 16.3% to 68.8% (52.5 points) against 19.0% to 52.5% (33.4 points)
  for the rule segments that contain active customers. The ML partition separates outcome
  risk more sharply, which is what a retention budget needs.
- The rules provide more granularity (8 segments) but less actionability: several rule
  labels are value tiers, not treatments.

The honest conclusion: **the rules are a good sanity check and a cheap first pass, but
they are not a substitute for the clustering** when the decision depends on separating a
large cooling base from a lapsed tail. The ML solution is not "better" in the abstract;
it is better aligned to the specific decision.

The full cross-tab is in `reports/figures/rfm_vs_clusters.png`.

## 8. Strategy matrix

These are illustrative expected values, not causal estimates. Each uplift is an explicit
assumption applied to the segment's **realised** next-90-day revenue from the snapshot's
label window; costs are assumed per customer. The purpose is to rank where effort is worth
testing, not to promise a return. Phase 7 replaces these assumptions with a proper
expected-value targeting model and an A/B test design.

| Segment | Action | Channel | Assumed uplift | Incremental | Campaign cost | Net | Priority |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Champions | Protect the account: named contact, early access, no blanket discount | Account management | 10% | £186,750 | £26,975 | **£159,775** | 1 |
| Cooling mid value | Reactivate before lapse: personalised reminder + time-limited offer | Email | 4% | £18,263 | £1,949 | **£16,314** | 2 |
| New / one-time | Convert: second-purchase incentive inside 30 days | Email + voucher | 5% | £9,771 | £1,422 | **£8,349** | 3 |
| Lapsed low value | Suppress from paid channels; email only, no incentive | Email | 0.5% | £547 | £81 | **£465** | 4 |
| Returns-heavy accounts | Remediate first: audit refund reasons and fix the product or service issue | Service recovery | 0% | £0 | £630 | **-£630** | 5 |

The ordering is not a surprise, but the magnitudes are: Champions are worth 10× the next
segment and 340× the lapsed tail. A blanket retention campaign that spreads budget evenly
across 5,251 customers is therefore mostly a transfer to customers who were going to buy
anyway. The returns-heavy group has negative expected net value as a campaign and should
be treated as an operational investigation, not a marketing audience.

## 9. Business reading

1. **Protect the 20% that funds the business.** 1,079 Champions generate 72.9% of lifetime
   revenue and 70.6% of the next 90 days. Their churn is 16.3% — the lowest of any group —
   but losing a handful of wholesale accounts moves more revenue than converting hundreds
   of one-time buyers. Service quality, not discounting, is the lever.
2. **The biggest addressable leak is the cooling mid-value base.** 1,949 customers, 19.6%
   of lifetime revenue, 26.3% still active, 44.8% churn among those active. This is the
   only large group where a reactivation sequence has both reach and value.
3. **Do not spend on the lapsed one-time tail.** 1,623 customers, 3.9% of lifetime revenue,
   3.3% active, £67 forward revenue per customer. At any realistic contact cost, paid
   acquisition of them is negative margin; email suppression is the correct action.
4. **New buyers are a conversion problem, not a retention problem.** 474 customers with a
   median tenure of 73 days, 84.6% active, 45.6% churn. The second purchase is the decision
   point, and it happens inside the first quarter.
5. **Treat returns as an operational signal.** 126 accounts have a median return rate of
   33.3% and only 8 distinct products. Their forward value is low because refunds consume
   it. The right first step is a refund-reason audit, not an offer.

## 10. Limitations

- **One snapshot.** The segmentation describes 2011-09-01. Membership will drift; the
  module can be rerun on any complete snapshot, but segment-by-segment drift over time is
  not measured here.
- **K-Means assumes roughly spherical, equal-variance clusters in the scaled space.** The
  features are correlated (frequency and revenue especially). The result is stable and
  interpretable, but the boundaries between adjacent value tiers are not causal.
- **Churn is a 90-day label.** The metric dictionary justifies 90 days from the purchase
  interval distribution, but a customer who would have returned in month 4 is counted as
  churned. Segment churn ranks are robust to the window; the absolute level is not.
- **Churn for lapsed segments is thin.** 53 active customers in Lapsed low value and 16 in
  Returns-heavy accounts. Those two rates are reported but not used to rank priorities.
- **Cadence is imputed for 30.2% of the base.** Single-purchase customers are assigned
  their tenure as an interval. This is defensible but it is an assumption, and it is
  flagged per customer.
- **Gross revenue ignores refunds in M.** Return behaviour is carried separately. A
  customer with £10k gross and a 90% return rate looks high-value on M; the returns signal
  is what identifies their segment.
- **The strategy uplifts are assumptions, not measurements.** They are deliberately
  transparent so they can be challenged. No causal claim is made, and no experiment has
  been run.
- **63 refund-only accounts are excluded.** They are not buyers, but they are real
  customers with service history; they belong in a service analysis, not a value
  segmentation.
